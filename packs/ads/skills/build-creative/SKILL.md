# Build creative

The ad account is not the lever. The winning creative is. You build a system that keeps producing winners: concepts, hooks, tests, teardowns and the next batch. Copy craft and the honesty floor are `write-ad-copy`; synthetic presenters are `produce-ai-ugc`; the account around the creative is `run-paid-social`. The pack floor in `PACK.md` applies throughout.

## Use this skill when

- Performance rises and falls with one winning ad.
- A new concept, hook set or variant batch is due.
- A test needs reading, or a winner needs mining.
- An organic feed needs trigger-moment posts and a moment bank.
- A hook must work in the first seconds with the sound off.

## First lesson: a system, not a tweak

Install a weekly creative system once results swing on one winner. Testing cadence scales with spend and with what you can produce. A two-person team that can make four distinct concepts a week runs a four-concept system, not a forty-concept one. Consistency beats bursts: a few distinct concepts every week, never a missed week, beats a one-time dump. Scaling spend without scaling creative production is the central failure.

Volume comes before reputation. Early on, ship to learn. Once the account has a reputation, publish when the work clears the value bar, not because the calendar says so. Make one source piece a week and repurpose it across formats rather than inventing fresh in every lane.

## Procedure

### 1. Test structure

One concept per ad set, a few hook variants of that concept: same body, different openings, especially different first seconds in image and message. Vary one thing so the read is clean. Testing ad sets stay small enough to exit learning; the platform names too many ads as a cause of learning limited (a dated row).

Never kill at 24 hours. The platform needs days to optimise. Do kill when a few days bring no spend at all. Read at a full week at low volume to clear day-of-week effects; read earlier only when volume is high. Pre-register the kill rule with denominators before launch.

### 2. Read outcome metrics first, story metrics second

Outcome metrics decide spend: spend, cost per result, conversions, return. Story metrics (hook rate, hold rate, click-through) diagnose why a creative wins or loses and tell you what to make next.

- Spend without conversion: the hook works; fix the back half, the script or the page.
- No spend: fix the hook.
- Low hook rate and low hold rate: the concept is dead; iterating hooks on it polishes a dud.
- Hook and hold high, click-through low: the creative grips the wrong people, or a narrow audience is exhausted. Rework the offer, the ask or the targeting signal, not the hook.
- A winner with a high hook rate: mine it; the hook is the transferable asset.

Treat cost per click, cost per thousand and click-through as barometers against your own baseline, not absolutes. There is no sourced "good hook rate"; write "assume ~X (validate)" if you must plan on one.

### 3. Mine winners in order: format, messaging, visuals last

When you find a winner, yours or a competitor's, take it apart in this order:

1. Format: testimonial, us-versus-them, listicle, demo, founder story, day in the life, native-UI mockup.
2. Messaging: primary text, on-creative text, headline, the core claim or mechanism.
3. Visuals last: the look is the least transferable, most copied, least causal part.

Translate the winning mechanism into a testable format for your own offer. Never rip the creative; you lose to the original. Swipe the reasoning, never the copy; the framework compounds across niches, the copied line does not.

Mine customer language: reviews, ad comments, support threads. Pull unusual or repeated phrases and turn them into headlines and on-creative text. A customer's own phrase, used word for word as a hook, is the highest-yield hook source there is.

Use the public ad library's longevity to choose what to study first. An ad that has run for months is one the advertiser keeps paying for: sustained use, not proven performance. The library shows no spend, no return, no budget. Rank hypotheses with it; never write a competitor's return from it.

Replicate, ideate, vary. If you lack taste, start with replication to build it, from direct competitors (same product) and indirect ones (same problem). Then most of each batch iterates winners and a smaller slice tests genuinely new messaging. New messaging changes one of: desire, awareness stage, sophistication, or persona. An iteration changes the actor, the setting, or the visual hook. A recolour is noise, not a variation.

### 4. Pick the awareness stage, then the hook type

Pick the market's awareness stage first and write to one stage. Mixing stages talks to nobody. The ladder, the hook-type menu with examples, the stacking rule and the prompt patterns are in `references/hook-menu.md`. To produce eight hooks fast, take eight different types and write one line each.

### 5. Trigger-moment posts and the moment bank

A trigger moment is an event that happens to many people every day or week, costs a named amount or loss, and that people describe in public soon after. A client refuses to pay an invoice. A tenant stops paying. A venue cancels the week before the event. The first tax bill lands bigger than expected. The post names the person, the event and the figure. The reader in that moment knows it at once, and the offer reads as the fix.

Build a moment bank before writing. Mine forum threads where people describe the event; threads with many first-person replies are richest, and the votes show which versions of the pain are most shared. One row per moment: the event, the typical figure or loss, the exact words people use, the source link, the fix they say they wanted, and later every post written from it with how it did. The words in the bank are the copy. On a vault, each row is a claim with its source.

One post, one moment: one person, one event, one figure, how it feels now, then the relief. Point every post at one destination that fixes that exact problem. Judge on paying buyers, not reach.

Catch the moment where it happens. A costly event often sends the person to search first. Run trigger-moment posts on a social platform only when the moment recurs across many new people and they already talk about it there. Otherwise the same bank feeds search pages and ad copy.

Reposting a real story is fine when the source is linked, with your own read added. Never present it as your own or a customer's experience.

### 6. The first seconds

The opening decides whether the ad makes money or burns it. It stops the human and gives the delivery system its signal. Test openings independently of the body.

A hook is visual plus text plus voice, never only a spoken line. Build three interrupts at once: a visual pattern break in frame one, the hook line on screen legible at a glance, and a spoken line that matches the text and the same desire. Write the text hook first, then ask how to show it.

Copy carries most of the hook's weight; the visual stops the scroll; audio moves the least. Build for sound off, then add the sound-on layer. The final check is literally watching the ad muted once: does it still stop the thumb, is the point clear? Never ship a hook that only works with the volume up.

Name the buyer or the problem early for cold traffic. No clever or delayed hooks for strangers; a low-conviction question or a generic callout loses the first seconds. Each platform publishes its own hook guidance (dated rows); carry them separately.

Plain beats polished. Audiences have learned to skip produced content. Run a few visually different formats at once (split-screen, listicle, user-style footage); the platform matches each format to its own audience pocket. The narrative structure that keeps winning: a specific panic moment, then relief. A structure that manufactures outrage to bait comments crosses the honesty floor; the tension arc without the bait is fine.

### 7. The native-UI mockup format

A container, not a hook type: the ad is a pixel-accurate recreation of a phone's own interface (a message thread typing itself in, a notification stack landing on a lock screen, a notes screen) with your message inside the native chrome. It reads as the viewer's own phone, so it stops the thumb like a real notification. The first bubble or banner is the hook, sound-off by construction. Pair it with a demographic or problem-led opener. It suits consumer, conversational angles far more than a formal services pitch, and it fatigues like any viral format; check it is still fresh.

Build the chrome by compositing real interface elements frame by frame, never by generating it. A video model smears interface type and icons, and that tell collapses the illusion. Keep the brand off the system chrome and on the end card. A dramatised conversation is fine; a fabricated "real customer" screenshot passed off as genuine is a fake testimonial.

### 8. Hook testing and the next batch

Produce a few openings on a stable body and let delivery surface the strongest. Read hook and hold rates after the outcome metrics.

Refresh cold-audience creative when frequency climbs or click-through drops week over week, after ruling out budget shifts, traffic quality, offer, season and tracking. Do not assume fatigue first.

A winner is a seed, not a finish line:

1. Preserve it. Keep the original running while it stays in goal; move it by post id into a winner ad set to keep its engagement.
2. Mine it: format, messaging, visuals.
3. Iterate from it: more hooks like it, a small share of new messaging.
4. Retire it before it decays. Rotate new concepts in; do not milk one body forever.

### 9. Widen with a model, judge with a human

Use a language model to widen the hook pool, not to replace judgment. The prompt patterns in `references/hook-menu.md` generate from a customer bio, rank for likely conversion, vary a winning transcript, and sharpen top hooks. The screen on every output is the honesty floor and the voice guide. Every hook that goes live passes the campaign's reviewer (`PACK.md`, review and send); AI-written hooks compete in the A/B with human ones on the outcome metric.

### 10. Name the offer by its outcome

Name the offer after the outcome and its parts, not the activity. Buyers pay for leads, not for advertising; for a full diary, not for scheduling. When an offer tires, refresh in order: creative, copy, headline and wrapper, season, duration, enhancer, structure last.

## Anti-patterns

- All hope in one hook. A hook is a hypothesis; test several.
- A hook that is only a spoken script.
- Clever or delayed openings for cold traffic.
- Writing to a topic instead of a moment: "tips for freelancers" reaches browsers; "the client who just refused to pay your invoice" reaches the person with the problem.
- A stranger's story told as your own.
- Recolour non-variations called tests.
- Scaling spend without creative.
- Treating data as the whole creative story, or all-model scripts with no human judgment.
- Fabricated proof, an undisclosed synthetic presenter where disclosure is required, manufactured shame, an outcome the product cannot deliver.

## Checks before you return

- Each test is one concept with a few hook variants, the kill rule written first.
- Outcome metrics were read before story metrics; the diagnosis names which.
- Every hook names its awareness stage and its type; the set spans several types.
- Every hook works muted, as on-screen text.
- The moment bank rows carry the source link and the exact words.
- SEND blocks are separate from PREP and pass the floor.
