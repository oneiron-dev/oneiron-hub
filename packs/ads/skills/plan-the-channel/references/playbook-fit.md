# Playbook fit: will this playbook work for this offer?

A growth playbook from a thread, a course or a case study is one offer, sold on one channel, by one operator, at one moment. It transfers when your offer matches it on the variables that made it work. It fails quietly when it does not. Take the playbook apart, read your offer on the same variables, then check the table. Most viral playbooks are sold by the person posting them. Their numbers are hypotheses until you have your own.

## 1. Take the playbook apart

Every playbook answers five questions. Write the five answers down before judging it.

1. Attention. What brings the stranger: a post, a search result, an ad, a message, a listing?
2. Trust. What makes them believe: a face, proof, reviews, specificity, a referral?
3. Close. What makes them pay: one link, a call, a trial, a checkout?
4. Price and decision. What price does it assume, and who decides: one person on a card, a considered purchase, a committee?
5. Operator load. What must someone do every week to keep it running?

Then write what it assumes without saying: where the buyer is when the need hits, how much trust the price needs, and what the listing platform does for it.

### Worked teardown: the "one tip a day" playbook

The playbook, as sold: a faceless page on a short-video platform posts one home-repair tip a day, mined from forum threads where homeowners describe a disaster. The page sells a repair checklist bundle through a marketplace listing. Reviews on the listing do the closing. The seller's thread claims the page "runs itself" and converts "at 4%".

| part | what the playbook says | what it assumes |
|---|---|---|
| attention | one short tip video a day, each about one costly mishap | homeowners scroll that platform near the moment of the mishap |
| trust | specificity ("this page knows my leak"), then the review count | an anonymous page earns enough trust at this price |
| close | one listing, one link in the bio | the marketplace's reviews and checkout do the selling |
| price | a one-off purchase under the price of a takeaway, one person decides | an impulse-sized purchase |
| operator load | a moment bank, a scheduler, follow-ups for reviews | the format works before it is automated |

Verdict: each part holds somewhere. Trigger moments make strong copy. Buyer density beats audience size. Reviews are proof. Cheap templates sell through marketplace search. The whole bundle, an anonymous page selling one listing, has no corroborated sales case; the located wins for products and services on social platforms are founder-led. The "4%" has no denominator (of views, of profile visits, of listing visitors?). Treat it as a test to run, not a system to copy. The test: a pre-registered window, a tagged link, a kill line in paying buyers.

## 2. Read the offer on the nine fit variables

1. Gross-profit ceiling. What a customer is worth after costs, over their life, sets what a channel may cost. A low-price one-off leaves little room for paid ads or selling by hand. Count a back end (upsell, repeat, subscription) only if it is measured.
2. Declared demand. After the problem appears, do people type it into a search box? Yes: search, SEO and AI answers catch them. No: discovery channels must create the awareness first.
3. Buyer density. Does the audience you can reach contain buyers at your price? Name the accounts, communities, keywords or lists. Peers and fans are not customers. For a social platform, pull a sample of recent posts in the problem's own words, large enough to count, and count the ones written by someone who has the problem. Vendors, commentators and news do not count.
4. Decision cost and trust. What does the buyer risk, who else must approve, and what proof or human contact removes the risk? A link and an anonymous page carry an impulse purchase. They do not carry a costly, expert-dependent or committee purchase.
5. Cycle length. A long decision needs repeated education and a follow-up path. A spike of visitors is not a pipeline.
6. Show-ability. Can a stranger see the benefit in the native format within seconds? Visible products suit video. Numbers, stories and arguments suit text.
7. Trigger timing and purchase frequency. What happened just before the purchase, how often it happens to new people, and whether the same buyer needs it again. A moment that strikes many new people daily can feed content. A rare, once-per-buyer purchase leans to search.
8. Operator capacity. Can the team feed the format and read the results every week? Scheduling test posts is fine. Scaling an unattended queue before a format works finds nothing, because nobody reads the results.
9. Retention and quality. Judge a channel by paying, retained customers and their value, not by views, followers or visits. Keep visibility, signups, revenue and profit apart.

The conditions, by offer:

- Cheap digital product. Marketplace search is the route with reported sales cases, where buyers search for the task. A checkout page alone does not show that the host's discovery brought the buyer. SEO works when the asset solves a searched job; traffic can grow far faster than sales. Founder-led text needs an audience that already holds buyers. Short-form needs a show-able topic. Paid needs an acquisition cost that fits the margin, or a measured back end.
- Consumer app subscription. App-store search has repeated discovery and install evidence; measure paid conversion and retention yourself. Short-form and creator programs need a show-able use case, a repeatable format and the capacity to manage creators, judged on retained paid users, never views. Paid social only when optimising to a trial or paid event behind a proven paywall. Founder-led text validates the problem and brings early downloads; it is not shown as a lasting subscription engine.
- High-ticket course or coaching. Trust comes from a visible expert: founder content, an engaged list with a teaching sequence, search authority. Paid only after organic proof and a tested follow-up funnel.
- B2B service or consultancy. Helpful participation, with a free diagnostic, inside the groups where buyers gather; SEO for acute service pain; researched outbound to named prospects; freelance-marketplace discovery. Founder-led text works only where the buyer talks there and the founder posts expert content and replies. Search ads work when the cost per click fits the engagement value.
- Self-serve SMB software. Founder-led text when followers share the problem; native demo and story posts in communities; SEO on high-intent pages; affiliates who already reach the same buyers. AI-answer visibility shows up as measurable referred signups. Launch spikes convert a small waitlist, not a pipeline.
- Developer tool, open source or API. Building in public where technical users gather; value-first answers in technical communities; implementation tutorials as SEO. Trending lists, integration directories and maker launches bring adoption spikes; paid conversion from them is unobserved, so measure it.
- Physical consumer product. Organic short-form that shows the product, creator seeding with free product, and lifecycle email (welcome, abandoned cart, post-purchase, win-back). Paid only with margins and blended measurement.

The faceless column is empty for every offer. The located wins are founder-led. A faceless page is a cheap, pre-registered test, not a default. Costly, expert-dependent or B2B purchases need a named person. Faceless pages can carry cheap, visible, low-risk purchases where the listing's reviews do the trusting.

## 4. Read a seller's own numbers

Numbers from someone selling the course are hypotheses. Check each one:

- The denominator. "Converts at 11%" of what: followers, people reached, profile visitors, listing visitors? Without it, the rate means nothing.
- The arithmetic. Recompute it. Yearly figures built on odd day counts, rates that only work on the best week, revenue ladders that never dip.
- The ledger. A review count or review rate needs eligible buyers, requests sent and reviews published over a stated window.
- Independence. One case retold by five channels is one case.
- Attribution. A revenue screenshot posted on a platform is not a sale made by that platform. Ask which channel the buyers came through, and how the seller knows.
- The tells. A conversion rate with no denominator; "runs while you sleep"; neat day-by-day revenue ladders; the fix for everything is the seller's product.

Keep the mechanism if it matches your variables. Drop the numbers.

## 5. Reviews as the closer, done honestly

Ask after the buyer has had the value. Test the timing for your product; it differs by weeks between an app, a physical product and a service. Log eligible buyers, requests sent and reviews published, so the rate is real. Follow the platform's review rules and never pay for praise.
