# Messaging checklist

The checks a page or a long ad passes before the reviewer sees it. Examples are written for this page, across varied products.

## Message completeness

Every sales message carries all of these. One missing and the buyer keeps cycling instead of deciding.

- One problem, specific and isolated.
- The solution as the mirror of that problem, not a description of the method.
- The target, in one real person's language.
- The trigger: what just happened that makes now the time.
- Objections brought into the message and answered one by one. Hidden objections make the buyer mull, stall and vanish.
- Testimonials that lead with the doubt, then what the buyer found.
- Risk reversal, covering the hidden risks too (looking foolish, a painful rollout).
- One uniqueness, used everywhere. If it works under a competitor's logo, it is not unique.

## Page skeleton and the fast-scroll test

Headline naming a pain or outcome; a subhead adding a new angle; a product visual with context or contrast; proof; one call to action. Test every section on a fast scroll: does the scanner catch what it is, why it helps, and what to do next?

Length is not minimal. Too little leaves objections live; too much paralyses. Say precisely what the buyer needs and cut everything else. One call to action by default. Mixed-readiness traffic (newsletter, social, docs) can earn one secondary ask ("watch a demo"); that is an exception, asserted not measured.

If the reader sees only the headlines and subheads, the story still has to hold.

## Headline mechanics, with examples

- Outcome, time frame, mechanism: `Dry basement in one weekend, with the trench-free drain.`
- How to X without Y: `How to close your books monthly without hiring a bookkeeper.`
- Category first: `A booking page for mobile dog groomers.` before any brand line.
- The reader's own words: `"I just need the van to start in January."` as a headline for a battery service.
- The denial hook: deny an outrageous implication so the reader imagines the stronger claim. Powerful, off-register for most B2B.
- Honest loss framing: `Every week the leak waits, the ceiling costs you {{real figure}}.` Only with a real figure; never invented scarcity.

Write to one awareness stage. In a sophisticated market, the subhead carries "without [the things you already tried]", ordered by how often buyers really tried them.

## Layering skeletons

All run hook, develop, ask.

- Product, benefit, reason why, offer, story. Each layer makes the claim easier to believe.
- Problem, insight, solution. The default long form when the goal is a change.
- Problem first: the specific problem, why it exists, its economic consequence, its emotional cost.
- Hook, body, close. The short-video structure; a platform convention, verify.

## Proof placement

- Pick the credibility source before writing: own experience, or a customer's result.
- Open each testimonial on the objection it answers.
- Match the people shown to the buyer.
- A founder story only as an example of the reader's change: "I was where you are, this went wrong, I tried everything, this changed it, here it is."
- B2B proof is a story the buyer can tell their board.
- Metrics-only proof when a client cannot be named.

## The three tests, applied

| line | picture it? | check it? | nobody else? | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `Trusted by thousands of teams` | no | no | no | cut |
| `The leading platform for modern finance` | no | no | no | cut |
| `Cut no-shows {{real %}} for a six-chair dental practice` | yes | yes | yes, if real | keep, with the real figure |
| `Your release notes written in ten minutes, not a Friday afternoon` | yes | yes | mostly | keep, if ten minutes is true |

## Voice-guide build

1. Collect real samples the subject wrote recently.
2. Draft three pieces in their voice on topics they really cover.
3. They mark what reads off: a word they never use, a rhythm, a closer.
4. Revise. Add each flagged phrase to the ban list only when they never use it in their real writing.
5. Repeat for at least five rounds. A stakeholder who wants to stop at round two because it "sounds fine" is the usual way a guide ships shallow.
6. Record the punctuation defaults (most guides ban the em dash), the sentence length, the openers and closers, and the words the writer reaches for.

## The machine-writing tells

The floor for every SEND block, before the voice guide's own list: promotional words ("groundbreaking", "stunning", "must-have"), the rule of three, "not just X but Y", stacked hedges ("could potentially help"), "-ing" tails ("...ensuring your team stays ahead"), vague attributions ("industry reports suggest"), false ranges ("from startups to enterprises"), abstract metaphor nouns ("north star", "flywheel"), fancy ways to say "is" ("serves as", "boasts"), feel-claims instead of what it does, chatbot closers, bold-label lists, title-case headers inside short pieces. Rebuild from the specifics; swapping one tell for another is not a fix.
